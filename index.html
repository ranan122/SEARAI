The gap

What content filters miss

Most testing tells a team what their product says. It rarely tells them whether it responds the way a child actually needs. That is hard to see from the inside, and it is often what surprises teams later.

Same question, different child

A four-year-old and a ten-year-old ask the same question for different reasons. The right answer is different too.

A friendly voice earns too much trust

Children over-trust a friendly voice. An AI that is always available and always agreeable can quietly crowd out the adults a child needs.

Some moments need a grown-up

Some moments should not be handled by an AI alone: fear, confusion, a secret, a request the child does not understand. A well-designed product knows these moments and knows how to bring a grown-up in.

Polite replies can still harm

The most dangerous responses are often the polite ones. A reply can pass every filter and still overwhelm a child, mislead her, or teach her to stop asking adults.

Decades of research on parent-child interaction tell us exactly what a responsive partner looks like at each age. Almost none of it has made it into how child-facing AI is built or tested.

Why now

The rules for child-facing AI are changing

California's Adam's Law (SB 1119) covers companion chatbots offered to users in California. Operators that allow children to use their chatbot must assess the risks to children and document how they reduce those risks before they release a new or substantially changed chatbot. Independent child safety audits follow.

Summary as of November 2026, based on this legal summary of SB 1119. SEAR AI does not give legal advice, and a developmental review is not the audit the law describes. It helps your team see and document how the product behaves with children.
